What should a warehouse-buying agent handle?

A commercial real estate agent should help you define what the warehouse needs to do for your business, then find and assess properties that fit. That means discussing the building's size and layout, access for trucks, loading and storage needs, parking, utilities, and room for future changes.

The agent can arrange property tours, communicate with the seller or listing representative, help prepare and negotiate an offer, and track key dates in the purchase process. Confirm exactly what representation includes, who the agent represents, and how the agent is compensated before you proceed.

An agent is not a substitute for a lawyer, inspector, environmental consultant, surveyor, or lender. A good process makes clear which questions the agent can help coordinate and which need an independent professional's review.

What should you check on a Longview warehouse?

Start with the property's permitted use and the way you plan to operate. Confirm the applicable jurisdiction and check zoning or other land-use requirements with the relevant local office. Rules and approvals can depend on the specific property and proposed use, so don't assume that a building's current use guarantees yours will be allowed.

Walk the site with your intended operations in mind. Look at truck turning and loading areas, driveway access, parking, ceiling height, floor condition, electrical capacity, water and sewer service, drainage, and the condition of the roof and other major systems. Ask what documentation is available for repairs, permits, utilities, and prior uses.

Review the survey, title information, easements, access rights, and any restrictions that could affect your plans. Depending on the site and its history, your due diligence may also include a flood-risk review, environmental assessment, building inspection, and confirmation of fire and life-safety requirements.

What affects the cost and timing?

There is no one standard cost for hiring an agent to buy a warehouse. Compensation can depend on the representation agreement and transaction, so ask how the fee is calculated, who pays it, when it is due, and whether any other charges may apply. Get the terms in writing before you commit.

Timing depends on how specific your requirements are, the number of suitable properties, seller response time, financing, and the amount of due diligence needed. A warehouse with incomplete records, questions about permitted use, repair concerns, or environmental issues may take longer to evaluate than a property with clear documentation.

  • The seller's availability and responsiveness during negotiations.
  • Financing approval, appraisal, title review, and survey availability.
  • Inspections, environmental review, utility checks, and any follow-up testing.
  • The purchase agreement's deadlines and how quickly each party provides documents.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Choosing a property based on floor area alone, without checking truck access, loading, utilities, and the fit with your day-to-day operations.
  • Assuming the warehouse's current use is approved for your intended use. Confirm requirements with the appropriate local authority before relying on that assumption.
  • Skipping inspections or accepting verbal assurances about roof condition, repairs, environmental history, or building systems.
  • Making an offer before understanding financing, due-diligence periods, deadlines, and the consequences of missing them.
  • Signing an agency agreement without understanding whom the agent represents, what services are included, and how compensation works.

Call a commercial real estate professional early if you're unsure how to compare properties, need help communicating with a seller, or want to understand the transaction steps before making an offer. Bring in the relevant independent specialists when a question involves legal, structural, environmental, financing, or regulatory advice.
