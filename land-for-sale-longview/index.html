What should land-buying help cover?

Land buying is different from choosing a finished home. A parcel can look suitable online but still need answers about legal access, boundaries, utilities, restrictions, drainage, or whether the intended use is allowed. A real estate professional can help identify questions, compare listings, arrange property visits, and guide communication around an offer and transaction.

Ask at the outset what the agency will handle and where another specialist needs to step in. A surveyor can address boundary questions, a title company can review title matters, and the relevant local authority can explain applicable rules. A listing description is a starting point, not proof that a particular use or improvement will be approved.

What should you check on a Longview-area parcel?

Start with the property's location and intended use. A tract within city limits may be subject to different requirements than land outside them, so confirm the jurisdiction and ask the appropriate office about zoning, permitting, subdivision, or other rules that could affect your plans. Do not assume that a neighboring property’s use means the same use is allowed on the parcel you’re considering.

  • Access: Confirm how the property connects to a public road and whether any private road or access easement is documented.
  • Boundaries: Review available survey information, corners, easements, and any uncertainty about the lines on the ground.
  • Services: Ask utility providers about service availability and connection requirements. If a private water or wastewater system is being considered, confirm site suitability and local requirements with the relevant professionals and authorities.
  • Ground and water: Look into drainage, low areas, flood information, and whether the land’s shape or soil may affect construction.
  • Ownership and restrictions: Have title and deed records reviewed for easements, restrictions, mineral interests, or other matters that could affect use.

These checks are especially useful before you settle on an offer amount or remove contract protections. Their answers can change which parcel is workable, even when two listings appear similar.

What affects the cost and timing of buying land?

Costs depend on the property and the transaction, so a buyer should ask for the proposed agency arrangement and transaction costs in writing rather than rely on a general estimate. The parcel’s size, location, access, services, permitted use, and condition can all affect its asking price and the amount of investigation needed. Surveying, title work, financing, inspections, and other professional services may also involve separate fees.

Timing can depend on how quickly the seller provides records, whether a survey or title issue needs attention, and how long local offices or service providers need to answer questions. Financing and contract deadlines matter too. A tract with unclear access or utility questions may take more work to evaluate than one with current documentation, though neither situation guarantees a particular timeline.

If the land is for a business, rental, or future development, explain that early. Texas Farms & Ranches offers commercial real estate agency and real estate rental agency services, so buyers can ask whether those services are relevant to their plans.

What mistakes should land buyers avoid?

  • Treating a listing’s acreage or boundary image as a substitute for a current survey.
  • Assuming road access, utility connections, or a particular use are available without checking.
  • Overlooking easements, deed restrictions, mineral interests, or other title matters.
  • Making an offer before understanding which due diligence questions need answers and when contract deadlines fall.
  • Comparing parcels by asking price alone instead of considering site work, services, access, and intended use.

A practical safeguard is to write down the use you have in mind, then verify each requirement against the specific tract. If an answer is uncertain, find out who can confirm it and what documentation is needed before you rely on it.

  • Who should confirm access, boundary, utility, title, and local-rule questions?
  • What information or documents should I review before making an offer?
  • How are agency compensation and other transaction costs explained in writing?
  • Who will keep me informed about deadlines and outstanding due diligence?
  • Can you explain what happens if an important property question remains unresolved?

The answers should make responsibilities clear, including which questions the agent can help organize and which require a qualified specialist or government office. Compare providers on those specifics rather than relying only on a general promise to help.
